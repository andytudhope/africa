# Metaphorically Speaking

I have not written down, in one place, my understanding of metaphor as the bridge between the sciences and the humanities. So, here it is.

My "201 Introduction to Quantum Mechanics" course is a good enough place to start. Our professor began the class with the comment that, "The electron is a metaphor for some aspect of reality no-one has ever directly perceived." 

His statement changed the course of my life. I ended up doing a Masters in English Literature, rather than Radioastronomy as a result of it.

I began studying applied mathematics and physics because I wanted to understand the universe, how it works, and how it all fits together and relates. Mathematics is the most succinct language for doing so, and physics is the study of its application in practice. I thought that at the beginning of my studies, and I still think that now.

However, metaphor informs all of this, which was a great surprise to me. The word 'metaphor' comes from Greek roots: 'meta' means beyond (or between), and 'pherein' means to carry. It is language which carries meaning beyond/between what it is meant to hold. The use of metaphor is a linguistic act that collapses separation: you are saying one thing IS another, that what appears to be different is actually one and the same. 

Which is fascinating, because language is (in many ways) what establishes difference in the first place! So, metaphor is the linguistic structure that negates the language it depends on. It is, like the notion of 'self', a recursive phenomenon, [a strange loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Am_a_Strange_Loop).

And it has its most potent application in mathematics, which is why I say it bridges the sciences and the humanities. The equals sign is the mathematical symbol for metaphor. It says that one thing IS another. Consider the famous equation:

$$
E = mc^2
$$

Here, we are saying that energy IS matter. Actually, we are saying more than that, more than could fit in a metaphor constructed from natural language, because we are stating not just that energy is matter, we are also stating the exact and reproducible conversion factor (the speed of light squared) which defines the relationship. And all this fits into 5 symbols! Hence what has been called "the unreasonable effectiveness" of mathematics.

This is also why my professor made the claim he did at the beginning of our introduction to Quantum Mechanics. If you grasp this fact about metaphor, then you can use it with clarity and an awareness of the limitations it implies. 
If we employ the metaphor, "An electron is a particle", we can study electrical conduction, the movement of discrete charges through a material. This is not an esoteric phenomenon: it is what allows electricity to flow through the wires in our homes.

If we employ the metaphor, "An electron is a wave", we can study chemical bonding, the interactions of electron wavefunctions that help explain why atoms bind together to form molecules. This is no more esoteric than electrical conduction: it explains why hydrogen and oxygen form water, and why the molecules of which we and everything around us are composed hold together.
Neither metaphor is sufficient on its own, and both have different limitations. It is the usefulness and limitations of each metaphor that makes quantum mechanics such an interesting field.

All of which leads us back to energy. Just because one metaphor helps us observe an aspect of the universe, it doesn’t mean there aren’t other useful metaphors too. Many facts can be true at the same time. Energy is matter. Also, energy IS frequency, and the conversion factor is Planck's constant. Stated formally it looks like this:

$$
E = hf
$$

People who tell you that, "The universe is just energy and frequency", do not understand this very elementary building block that comes before actual quantum mechanics. Energy and frequency are not different things that need to be combined with 'and'. 

Next, we can consider the First Law of Thermodynamics: "Energy is neither created nor destroyed." The total energy in the universe IS a constant. In a universe of constant change, there is something which does not change and to that, physics assigns the label "total energy". 

While the total energy in the universe does not change, specific energy in particular contexts is constantly changing, a fact that finally leads us to an actual quantum equation:

$$
\Delta E\,\Delta t \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}
$$

The greater than symbol here is not quite the clean metaphor I have been discussing so far. But, on reflection, this makes perfect sense because we are looking at an equation which (ironically) defines uncertainty. We have been talking about how each metaphor we use has limits, and now we are looking at a metaphor about limitation itself. What we can know about how energy changes is limited by what we know about the time it takes to do so. The nature of the limit is nuanced, because time is not a quantum variable like position or momentum, but that there is a limit to what we can know at any given moment is clearly stated here in physical form. And thus the quantum world begins…

It is no coincidence that I have on my desk a book whose subtitle is "The book of changes and the unchanging truth" whose contents revolve around an ancient binary system of expression that the author explicitly calls "an energy language". This knowledge is neither secret nor suppressed: it has been expressed for a long time in various different ways. At base, it is very simple. It's just that we seem to have an inclination to embellish our metaphors in the hope that they can carry meaning not just beyond/between what they are meant to hold, but all the way to some "Universal Truth".

And this is where the bridge needs to take us back into the humanities, which is to say back into the study and living recognition of our limited nature as embodied beings experiencing space and time. Universal Truth of the sort we might like to impose on any other being is an asymptote: it cannot be reached. You can, at best, realise it silently within your own heart and then eternally refine your patience while relating to all the "others" you experience.

If there is any valid philosophical take-away from quantum mechanics, I think it [revolves around how we relate](https://www.kernel.community/en/learn/module-metta/relating-kernel) and the responsibility we are endowed with by virtue of being observers capable of self-reflection. And so we come to the final part of my academic life.

## Sincerity and Singularity

Having been immersed in maths and physics, I was fascinated with singularity (which has a different meaning in both fields). In maths, a singularity is something like the point (1,2) on a Cartesian plane: it exists, but it has no dimension. In physics, a singularity is something like the “event horizon” of a black hole: a place where the laws of physics cease to operate. In other words, [a place where place ceases to have meaning](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gHQ13miMkjE).

Then, I discovered that literature also has singularities: the moment a reader encounters a text is singular. You have never been, and will never again be, as you are right now, reading these words. And so the words only mean as they do right now, not before and never again. I wanted to know more about this, and how it related to truth. 

If each encounter between reader and text is singular, how can meaning be shared? How is any metaphor stable? My claim is that all such questions boil down to sincerity.

Sincerity is a theatrical term and it means to whole-heartedly play a role. To play it with such veracity that the audience must suspend disbelief and see you as the character you inhabit. To be sincere is the psychological equivalent of being in a quantum superposition. Similarly, to write sincerely, you must be totally honest about your particular truth and you must anticipate how any reader is likely to respond. To read sincerely, you must put aside suspicion and ask how the particular truth you are encountering is reflected in your life and you must remain skeptical of any truth claims you are presented with. Thus, at the meeting point of two particular histories/personalities/trajectories, something shared can emerge.

In other words, sincerity is the fundamental acceptance of, or surrender to, who you are right now. So, just like metaphor is the linguistic phenomenon which depends on and negates the language from which it is constructed, sincerity both depends on and simultaneously negates the "you" you think you are. To be sincere is, at once, to realise that there is only one Life and to live your life fully, as one among infinitely many.

Sincerity is the psychological structure that mirrors the linguistic structure of metaphor. Both are powerful because they put together what appears to be different, and so carry meaning (or words, or of your personality) beyond/between where it can ordinarily reach. 

Imagine my surprise when I began reading the Qur’an and came across [Surah Ikhlas](https://quran.com/al-ikhlas) (or, The Chapter of Sincerity) which is all about singularity. The first line invites you explicitly into the singularity each and every moment, in truth, presents you with. The last line is the end of metaphor. When I truly read it, I was finally and utterly content. 

It was a “quantum” realisation. Just as an electron is a particle and a wave, I am me and I am That. These are useful metaphors in different contexts: they reveal different aspects worthy of study and practical application. They are not mutually exclusive, nor are they exhaustive.

Any such system of metaphors will always be incomplete. We've proven that, too. There are sentences, like the last line of Surah Ikhlas, that can be expressed but never proved or disproved using the axioms from which they are constructed (in this case, the rules of language).

You are invited to meet directly that which has no equivalent. Metaphors are no help in the face of such an encounter. “Nothing could enter it, but the sincere act”, as Muhammad Niffari tells us. And so the academic chapter of my life closed. The bridge, after all, is not the destination.
